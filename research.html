What Connects Local Housing Markets? Regulation, Migration and the National Cycle
Job Market Paper · Accepted for presentation at the 2026 SMU–Jinan Conference on Urban and Regional Economics, joint with the 11th WB–GWU Urbanization and Development Conference (December 2026)
Abstract

Housing cannot be traded across regions, yet U.S. house prices have become far more synchronized. I estimate directed networks of house-price forecast-error dependence for the 48 contiguous states and the 100 largest metropolitan areas, 1985–2025, with and without the common factor of each ten-year window. The rise in connectedness is almost entirely that factor, which has grown from a fifth to two-thirds of the variance of state price growth, and dependence beyond it has not grown. Beyond the national cycle, land-use regulation lines up with how much dependence runs through a market and migration with where it comes from. A one-standard-deviation higher regulation index is associated with 7.5 log points more of other states' forecast-error variance attributed to a state. Regulated metropolitan areas take more of their variance from other areas where their own labor demand is rising, as a binding supply constraint implies, and which out-of-state areas that dependence comes from lines up with migration links.

JEL: R31, R23, C32, C33 · Keywords: house prices, connectedness, migration networks, spatial equilibrium, housing supply

Lost Wages and House Prices: The Oil Bust Where Norway's Offshore Workers Lived
October 2026
Abstract

What does a loss of wages do to a local economy? This paper uses Norway's offshore oil workers and the 2014 oil price fall as a quasi-experiment that separates a wage shock from a shock to local firms. Because these workers spend four weeks at home for every two on the platform, nearly half of them live in municipalities with almost no oil firms. I compare 375 such municipalities in a difference-in-differences design by their share of employed residents who worked offshore in 2003. From 2013 to 2017, income from employment fell by 1.9 percent, employment by 1.4 percent and house prices by 7 percent per percentage point of that share. The housing stock and the population did not move. The oil suppliers in these municipalities lost jobs but do not account for the price fall. House prices absorbed about four times as much of the shock as income did.

JEL: R31, R23, J23, Q33 · Keywords: house prices, local labor markets, oil price shock, offshore workers, income shocks, Norway

How Should a Penalized VAR Be Tuned to Measure Connectedness?
Preliminary draft, October 2026
Abstract

Connectedness indexes built from elastic-net VARs depend on a penalty that is usually chosen by ten-fold cross-validation with folds assigned at random. With persistent series, an observation held out for validation keeps its neighbours in the training sample, so cross-validation overstates how well a dense model predicts. In a Monte Carlo with a known sparse network, I compare seven ways of assigning observations to folds, two points on the cross-validation curve, information criteria and a plug-in penalty. When the median first autocorrelation is 0.88, random folds keep three times as many cross-lag coefficients as the best predicting penalty, add 35 percent to the error in pairwise connectedness and raise the index by about nine points. Folds separated by a gap of six to twelve observations, or a rolling origin, stay within 5 percent of the best penalty at every persistence level. Choosing the one-standard-error penalty is no remedy. Whatever the rule, about 100(N−1)/(T+N−1) points of a reported index come from noise in the estimated shock covariance alone.

JEL: C32, C52, C53, C15 · Keywords: connectedness, elastic-net VAR, cross-validation, persistence, forecast error variance decomposition, Monte Carlo
