What Connects Local Housing Markets? Land-Use Regulation and the National Cycle
Job Market Paper · Accepted for presentation at the 2026 SMU–Jinan Conference on Urban and Regional Economics, joint with the 11th WB–GWU Urbanization and Development Conference (December 2026)
Abstract

House prices move more where housing supply is restricted, by land-use regulation or by a shortage of buildable land. I ask whether those movements stay in the market or whether restricted markets also send price shocks to other markets. I measure Diebold–Yilmaz connectedness among the 48 contiguous U.S. states over 1995–2019, the share of each state's unexpected price movement over the following year that is traced to shocks in each other state. I estimate it on raw monthly price growth and on price growth with the national cycle removed. A state one standard deviation higher on the Wharton regulation index sends about 33 percent more to the other states on raw growth and about 24 percent more with the national cycle removed. What it receives does not rise significantly. The coefficient holds for a given receiving state at a given distance, and it is largest in cycles longer than four years. A shortage of buildable land is unrelated to sending on both networks.

JEL: R31, R23, C32, C33 · Keywords: house prices, connectedness, housing supply, land-use regulation

Oil Paychecks and House Prices: Norway's Offshore Bust in the Regions Where the Workers Lived
November 2026
Abstract

What does a loss of income do to regional house prices? When the oil price fell in 2014, Norway's offshore crews, who work two weeks on the continental shelf and four weeks at home, brought the shock to their home regions mainly through their paychecks. I use a difference-in-differences design with a continuous treatment across 70 economic regions without an onshore oil industry. The treatment is the share of a region's employed residents who worked offshore in 2003, the last year before the oil boom. I follow the outcomes from 2009 to 2019 relative to 2013. Per percentage point of offshore work, the price per square meter of detached houses fell 8 percent by 2017 and 9 by 2019, about as much as the boom had added. By 2019, two years after the paychecks had stopped falling, the price had not recovered. The loss I can measure behind the prices is the paycheck. The same share that brought paychecks in the boom took them away in the bust, 0.6 per 100 employed residents by 2017, with none returned by 2019. Pay per employed man fell about 2 percent and stayed down even as pay per employed woman rose. Employment, population and the dwelling stock did not move. With no fall in building through 2017, the loss showed up in prices.

JEL: R23, R31, J31, Q33 · Keywords: house prices, income shocks, offshore workers, local labor markets, oil bust, Norway

How Should a Penalized VAR Be Tuned to Measure Connectedness?
Preliminary draft, October 2026
Abstract

Connectedness indexes built from elastic-net VARs depend on a penalty that is usually chosen by ten-fold cross-validation with folds assigned at random. With persistent series, an observation held out for validation keeps its neighbours in the training sample, so cross-validation overstates how well a dense model predicts. In a Monte Carlo with a known sparse network, I compare seven ways of assigning observations to folds, two points on the cross-validation curve, information criteria and a plug-in penalty. When the median first autocorrelation is 0.88, random folds keep three times as many cross-lag coefficients as the best predicting penalty, add 35 percent to the error in pairwise connectedness and raise the index by about nine points. Folds separated by a gap of six to twelve observations, or a rolling origin, stay within 5 percent of the best penalty at every persistence level. Choosing the one-standard-error penalty is no remedy. Whatever the rule, about 100(N−1)/(T+N−1) points of a reported index come from noise in the estimated shock covariance alone.

JEL: C32, C52, C53, C15 · Keywords: connectedness, elastic-net VAR, cross-validation, persistence, forecast error variance decomposition, Monte Carlo
