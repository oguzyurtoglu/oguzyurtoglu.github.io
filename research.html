Working Papers

What Connects Local Housing Markets? Regulation, Migration and the National Cycle
Job Market Paper · Accepted for presentation at the 2026 SMU–Jinan Conference on Urban and Regional Economics, joint with the 11th WB–GWU Urbanization and Development Conference (December 2026)
Abstract

Housing cannot be traded across regions, yet U.S. house prices have become far more synchronized. I estimate directed networks of house-price forecast-error dependence for the 48 contiguous states and the 100 largest metropolitan areas, 1985–2025, with and without the common factor of each ten-year window. The rise in connectedness is almost entirely that factor, which has grown from a fifth to two-thirds of the variance of state price growth, and dependence beyond it has not grown. Beyond the national cycle, land-use regulation lines up with how much dependence runs through a market and migration with where it comes from. A one-standard-deviation higher regulation index is associated with 7.5 log points more of other states' forecast-error variance attributed to a state. Regulated metropolitan areas take more of their variance from other areas where their own labor demand is rising, as a binding supply constraint implies, and migration links line up with which out-of-state areas that dependence comes from.

JEL: R31, R23, C32, C33 · Keywords: house prices, connectedness, migration networks, spatial equilibrium, housing supply

Adverse Labor Shocks and House Prices: Evidence from Norway's Offshore Workers
October 2026
Abstract

The studies of the 2014 oil price bust measure the shock where the oil industry is located, so the workers and the firms of a region are hit together. I measure it where the workers lived. Norwegian offshore workers fly to the platforms from five heliports and spend two weeks offshore and four at home, so nearly half of them live outside the two oil counties, in municipalities with almost no oil firms. The bust reached these places mostly through the wages of their residents. Using public municipal data from Statistics Norway, I compare the 375 municipalities without an onshore oil economy by the share of their employed residents who worked offshore in 2003. Per percentage point of that share, the average income from employment fell by 1.9 percent between 2013 and 2017, employment of residents by 1.4 percent and the average sale price of dwellings by 7 percent. The dwelling stock and the population did not move. The effects sit on the region where offshore workers live, and the oil firms, the coastal industries and the equipment suppliers left in the sample do not explain them. The loss of income was the men's, and for each offshore job lost the equipment suppliers of the industry lost about one more and the rest of the local economy about two, imprecisely estimated. Lost wages move house prices in places with almost no oil firms.

How Should a Penalized VAR Be Tuned to Measure Connectedness?
Preliminary draft, October 2026
Abstract

Connectedness indexes built from elastic-net VARs depend on a penalty that is usually chosen by ten-fold cross-validation with folds assigned at random. With persistent series, a month held out for validation keeps its neighbours in the training sample, so cross-validation overstates how well a dense model predicts. In a Monte Carlo with a known sparse network, I compare seven ways of assigning months to folds, two points on the cross-validation curve, information criteria and a plug-in penalty. When the median first autocorrelation is 0.88, random folds keep three times as many cross-lag coefficients as the best predicting penalty, add 35 percent to the error in pairwise connectedness and raise the index by about nine points. Folds separated by a gap of six to twelve months, or a rolling origin, stay within 5 percent of the best penalty at every persistence level. Choosing the one-standard-error penalty is no remedy. Whatever the rule, noise in the estimated shock covariance alone puts a floor of about 100(N−1)/(T+N−1) points under the index.
